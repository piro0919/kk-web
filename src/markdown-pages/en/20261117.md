It has been about two or three months since I released a SaaS called [Spatto](https://spatto.app).

Spatto is the first B2B SaaS I have ever built on my own time.

From planning to development to promotion, I did almost all of it by myself.

So this time, I want to write about what I went through building a B2B SaaS alone for the first time.

---

Before getting into the main topic, let me briefly explain what the service is.

It is a service that uses AI to build shift schedules.

It started with a chat at the hair salon I always go to. The person who usually cuts my hair told me, "I tried to make a shift schedule with ChatGPT, but it just would not work, so I ended up giving up."

Then it hit me: what if I built it as a SaaS? That might be fun. And so I started building.

Spatto gives each shop two apps on separate domains, one for the owner and one for the staff.

Staff post their availability for the next month in the app, and once everyone has posted, the owner runs the AI and the shift schedule gets built automatically.

With that in mind, here is what I felt did not go well.

---

## A product that needs several people is hard to start using

This was the biggest blind spot after release.

Spatto is free for the first month, but to use it, not only the owner but also the staff have to start using the app.

Which means an owner cannot just casually try it out... and that is a real problem.

I have had the owner of that hair salon try it too, but asking the staff to start using it seems to be a tough ask. Well, of course it is.

I think it should have worked for a single person first.

Once I noticed this, I changed it so the owner can use it alone, but I feel that cuts the value of the service in half.

Finding the right target was hard.

## Nobody uses it, nobody buys it

Spatto supports multiple languages. That said, only Japanese and English.

So I promoted it not only in Japan but around the world. I will keep the methods to myself here.

But unfortunately, after spending some money and a lot of effort, there are only a handful of sign-ups once you leave out the demo shops and people I know.

## The guilt of not being a company

I released Spatto as a sole proprietor, and not being a company does leave me feeling a little guilty.

The clearest example is the legal notice that Japan's Act on Specified Commercial Transactions requires.

A company can just list its office address, but for a sole proprietor, what goes there is your home address and phone number.

I really wanted to avoid that, so I left them out and wrote that I will disclose them without delay on request.

The Consumer Affairs Agency allows this, but from the user's side, it is a business with no address and no phone number.

I am not doing anything wrong, but if I imagine a company talking about bringing in Spatto, it is in the end a service released by one individual, so it would be a hard sell.

There is no doubt that trust looks very different for an individual and a company.

## Turning a profit is hard

The service builds shift schedules a month at a time, so the AI costs are quite high.

So when you hear 500 yen per user per month, you might think "that is pricey!", but even that is cutting it close, and depending on how it is used, it honestly runs at a loss.

I have put a cap on AI costs, but I do not limit the cost of each time staff rewrite their availability, so if they rewrite it many times, it runs at a loss.

These days SaaS products are expected to have AI built in, so the quality of the product has gone up, but I get the strong impression that running costs have jumped too.

---

On the other hand, here is what went well.

## The fun of building, and the responsibility of being used

It goes without saying, but since I get to decide everything about the product, what is in my head goes straight into the product, and nothing is more fun than that.

I built it as a B2B SaaS, but for me the goal was to build it, so I think it was definitely good that I got there.

On top of that, what felt unique to B2B was a sense of responsibility.

I did not feel it much when I built consumer services, but with B2B, it gets used for a company's actual work.

So throughout development, there was always a slight feeling of tension right beside me.

For example, when the AI builds a schedule, it sometimes tries to fill short-staffed days by scheduling someone past the 40-hour work week set by Japan's Labor Standards Act.

If I built it to push past that automatically, it would become a tool that breaks the law by a fixed procedure.

So when it goes over, the screen says so, and the owner decides whether to go over.

---

In an age where people say "SaaS is dead," if you are well known, people will use what you make, and if you are not, you barely get the chance to be noticed.

If this is the result of running and running and running away from building my own brand, I do not really have any regrets worth calling regrets.

Even so, learning that something can go this unused was a really good lesson.

That said, it is also true that I found out how fun building a B2B SaaS is.

I plan to keep turning ideas into things.
